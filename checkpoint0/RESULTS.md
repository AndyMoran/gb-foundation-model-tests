# Checkpoint 0: Chronos-2, a weather regression and LightGBM on equal inputs, a backtest first look

Andy Moran (Heaviside Analytics) · 16 November 2026 · exploratory, descriptive

**What this does not show.** These are backtest days the foundation models may have seen in training, so this is not live performance. LightGBM is untuned, so this is not the best a trained model can do. It compares five forecasters, in eight configurations, on one series, GB national demand (INDO) a day ahead, so it does not rank model families. ## Headline

In a backtest the foundation models may have seen in training, Chronos-2 forecast GB demand 19.3% more accurately than a LightGBM model with library defaults, on equal inputs. The LightGBM model was 11.8% less accurate than a weather regression given the same information: with library defaults, the trained tree model did not beat the linear one. A live, preregistered test starts on 1 April 2027.

Both sentences were generated by rules fixed in the plan before any LightGBM run (`PLAN.md` §7), including the form for LightGBM losing to the regression.

**In context.** The tougher opponent was the regression, not LightGBM. Against it, Chronos-2's lead is about 10%: 916 against 1,014 MW of daily error, the weather study's E5 result, reproduced here exactly. A per-slot linear regression with temperature terms is a well-known strong baseline in load forecasting. Hong, Wang and Willis's "vanilla" benchmark, built the same way, was used as a benchmark in the GEFCom2012, 2014 and 2017 competitions. So the regression's strength is consistent with the field, not an anomaly. This is a finding about LightGBM's defaults and model structure on this information, not about LightGBM as a library. The outcome was not foregone: a 2026 benchmark of household electricity forecasting found LightGBM the most reliable and accurate of its trained models (Dube et al., 2026). That is why it was the trained baseline here.

## What was compared

Every forecaster had the same information: demand to 08:30 on D−1, national temperature, wind and solar as forecast at the 09:30 issue time, and the calendar. Two configurations have no calendar. TimesFM 2.5 was given none, because its eight-week context holds at most one Christmas period and few bank holidays, too few to learn their effect. Timestral is also shown with weather only (see below). The backtest is the weather study's 960 days, 16 March 2024 to 31 October 2026.

- **Chronos-2, Timestral, TimesFM 2.5:** zero-shot, the weather study's saved forecasts, checked against their hashes.
- **Weather regression:** 48 per-slot models with temperature, its square, wind, wind chill and solar, refitted daily on the year to D−2.
- **LightGBM (new):** one model across all half-hour slots, given exactly the regression's information (slot, calendar, demand at D−2 and D−7, temperature, wind, solar), refitted daily on the same window. Library defaults, except an absolute-error objective, a fixed seed and day of week as a category. Defaults are not neutral: on 58 time series, randomly generated configurations cut LightGBM's mean squared error by up to 17.45% against the default (Barros, Cerqueira and Soares, 2021; from the paper's abstract, as the full text was not accessible). So two sensitivity runs used configurations published for load forecasting, unchanged except for the objective:
  - **S1:** Misiurek, Olkuski and Zyśk (2026): 500 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, depth 8, row and column sampling 0.8.
  - **S2:** Uzzaman et al. (2026): 500 trees, learning rate 0.03, 31 leaves.
- **Timestral is shown both ways and not ranked:** a known defect affects its calendar input (`mistralai/timestral#9`).

Ratios are each forecaster's mean daily error over the comparator's. Intervals are 90%, by moving-block bootstrap over days (blocks of 7, 2,000 draws). A difference is described only where the ratio's interval excludes 1.

|---|---|---|
| Chronos-2 (calendar and weather) | 916 | 0.807 (0.778–0.837) |
| Weather regression | 1,014 | 0.894 (0.869–0.920) |
| LightGBM, defaults | 1,134 | 1 |
| TimesFM 2.5 (weather), corrected² | 1,620 | 1.428 (1.365–1.488) |
| Timestral, calendar and weather (not ranked) | 1,103 | 0.973 (0.942–1.006) |

**The sensitivity settings do not change the reading.** With S1, LightGBM's error falls to 1,061 MW and with S2 to 1,094 MW, but both still trail the regression (1.046, 1.023–1.070; and 1.079, 1.052–1.107). Chronos-2 still leads each (0.863 and 0.836).

## Why LightGBM lost: two hypotheses, not tested here

- **Extrapolation.** A tree predicts only values it has seen, in steps. On cold days at the edge of its training data, it cannot follow the steady rise in demand that the regression's temperature terms capture. This limitation is documented. In load forecasting, gradient-boosted trees are reported to predict high loads poorly, and "model extrapolation is tough for such tree-based ensemble learners as regression tree usually assigns a constant for a split feature space" (Yang et al., 2023). More generally, standard tree models "are not adept at forecasting beyond the range of the data they have been trained on" (März and Rasul, 2024). The winter pattern is consistent with this; this study does not test it.
- **Structure.** The regression fits 48 per-slot models with explicit temperature, squared-temperature and wind-chill terms. LightGBM fits one model across all slots, and must discover those shapes itself. S1 and S2 changed its size and learning rate, not that structure. Five times the trees (S1) did help, from 1,134 to 1,061 MW.

They lead to different designs. Extrapolation argues for keeping the regression as the main trained benchmark. Structure argues for a per-slot LightGBM, or one given the regression's temperature terms.

## What checkpoint 1 should test first

**Proposed for checkpoint 1's plan, before any tuning:**
1. **A pre-specified per-slot LightGBM.** That tests the structure hypothesis.
2. **Each forecaster's error on the highest-demand days, for example the top 10% by outturn.** That tests the extrapolation hypothesis, which predicts LightGBM's shortfall is concentrated there rather than spread across winter.

Neither was run here, because the plan's analysis budget ends at its own tables.

## What it means for the live benchmark

- **The trained benchmark is weaker than assumed.** Given only the regression's information and default settings, LightGBM is the easier bar. Checkpoint 1's plan must decide its trained baselines with that in mind.
- **The Chronos-2–LightGBM pair is unlikely to limit power.** On summer days, Chronos-2's edge over LightGBM is 14.9% (90% interval 12.4–18.8%), with an error correlation of 0.59, larger than its edge over the regression. Both are backtest figures. Gate G2's power calculation, at the central edge and at the interval's low end, is a separate pre-specified step. The regression pair is likely to be the binding one.
- **Winter matters.** The trained model is weakest in winter, the season the live benchmark cannot test confirmatorily. Winter tables will be prominent in every checkpoint report.
- **Mean daily error is not the whole story.** It is the metric this study was scored on, but for a system operator or a trader the cost of error concentrates on the most extreme days, where a model that cannot extrapolate is most likely to fail. Error on the highest-demand days is the second test proposed above.
- **Two benchmark rules come from this study.** Table 5's split at Chronos-2's release is the prototype of draft rule 4.4: each model is scored only on days after its release or stated training cut-off. The TimesFM correction led to draft rule 4.8: every model forecasts one day per call, and a call-mode check before entry confirms that batching changes nothing (`BENCHMARK_RULES.md`).
- **NESO is still well ahead,** at about 700 MW against Chronos-2's 916. NESO has richer weather, embedded generation estimates and forecasters' judgement. This benchmark asks which method makes best use of public data, not whether any matches a system operator.

## Deviations and corrections

The plan was frozen at tag `checkpoint0-plan` before any LightGBM code ran. All five pre-run checks passed first time, and scoring used the committed `score.py`, unchanged. Every change is logged in [`REVISIONS.md`](REVISIONS.md). The ones that matter:

- **Table 5's split date.** The plan gave Chronos-2's release as 20 October 2025, and also said to read the table as the weather study's leakage check, which used 30 October. The two conflict. 30 October was used, before any run.
- **The cost runner** forecasts one day per call and gives each forecaster its own process. The plan named neither choice.
- **² TimesFM 2.5 corrected.** The cost check found that TimesFM's weather-study forecasts depended on the other days in each 64-day batch, including later days' demand. Weather study correction 1 (DOI [10.5281/zenodo.24284719](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24284719)) remade them one day per call. The behaviour is reported upstream as [google-research/timesfm#577](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm/issues/577). `results.md`, as scored, shows TimesFM at 1,474 MW (ratio 1.299). The corrected rows (`results_correction1.md`) show 1,620 MW (1.428), and that is what the table above reports. In cost, the batched 0.33 seconds a day was the flawed setup; one day per call costs 10.63.
- **A false warning.** The cost run's closing warning counted the one-day TimesFM row, which is not meant to match the batched forecasts. The fix changed only the message.
